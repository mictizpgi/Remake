01Situation

Beds were the bottleneck.

This medical center turns over roughly 30,000 rooms a year. Every one of those turns runs through Environmental Services (EVS): a patient leaves, the room is flagged dirty, a housekeeper is assigned, the room is cleaned, and the next patient moves in.

Across the region, that wait for a housekeeper averaged 1 hour and 13 minutes. Here it was 1 hour and 32. Clean times were among the longest in the region too.

02Problem

The work was hiding in the handoffs.

Let me share what the data and the floor showed us...

  • The bed board wasn’t watched often enough, so dirty rooms sat in the queue
  • Housekeepers arrived to find IV poles, trays, and meds still in the room
  • Rooms weren’t always flagged dirty when the patient left. 6,113 rooms sat “hidden” for 37 extra minutes on average
  • Discharges peaked at 11am, but the discharge cleaning crew started at 4pm
  • Urgent STAT requests pulled housekeepers out of rooms they were halfway through cleaning

None of this was a people problem. Each department was doing its job. The gaps were between them.

03Action

Six small tests of change.

We built a dashboard fed daily from the hospital’s records system, so everyone was looking at the same numbers. Then we got out of the conference room and into the department, and brought Nursing, bed control, case management, and transport to the table.

  1. 1

    Eyes on Board.

    EVS managers took turns checking the bed board about every 30 minutes, every shift.

  2. 2

    Room Ready swarm.

    Nurses cleared patient items from the room before discharge, so housekeepers could start right away.

  3. 3

    Wristband trigger.

    Cutting the patient’s wristband became the cue to flag the room dirty.

  4. 4

    Staggered shifts.

    Working with EVS leaders and union stewards, we moved discharge crew start times earlier to meet the peak.

  5. 5

    STAT assignments.

    EVS managers, who can see the whole floor, took over assigning urgent rooms.

  6. 6

    Same-unit transfers.

    A simple approval step for in-unit room moves, which had cut them about 20% in an earlier pilot.

04Result

Each fix stacked on the last.

The wait for a housekeeper dropped 26 minutes, and still held at 22 through the surge. Clean times came down 2 minutes a room. On the pilot unit, rooms flagged late went from waiting 39 minutes to 21.

Added up across a year, that’s 15,963 hours of patient capacity, or 665 more patient days, and more than $1.3M in soft savings. EVS also got back 1,514 hours of staff time, enough to clean about five more rooms a night, and STAT room requests fell 60%, from 10% of assignments to 4%. That’s roughly 1,700 fewer interruptions a year.

Then we looked past the pilot. Applied across the health system’s region, the same results work out to about 5,000 hours of patient capacity for every 100 beds. That gave leadership a model other medical centers could follow.

All of this happened while a surge in patients was hitting the hospital hard. Five of the six pilots stayed in place anyway.

Small, steady changes hold up under pressure.
